Volume Shader Test: How to Run It and Read Your Score

Updated 2026-10-21 · 7 min read · by the VolumeShader team

The volume shader test gives you FPS, frame time, a 1% low value and a score in less than a minute. The numbers only help if you know how to run the test fairly and how to read them. This guide covers both, with simple examples.

Before you press start

The same GPU can give very different results depending on how you prepare. A little care here makes your numbers useful, especially if you want to compare two devices or compare the same device on two different days.

  • Plug in a laptop. Many laptops lower GPU power on battery to save energy. That alone can change the result a lot.
  • Close other tabs and apps. Videos, games, screen recording and many open tabs share the GPU with the test.
  • Turn off battery saver. On phones and tablets, power saving modes often limit performance.
  • Use a cool device. If your phone has just been charging or playing a game, wait a few minutes so the first result is not already affected by heat.
  • Use the same browser every time. If you compare two devices, use the same browser on both when you can.

If you are not sure the browser is even using your GPU, open our WebGL diagnostics page first. It shows the renderer name. A name that mentions SwiftShader, llvmpipe or "software" means the browser is using the CPU, and the test will run very slowly.

Choosing the resolution and preset

On the home page you can pick the size of the canvas and the shape to draw. Both change how heavy the test is.

ResolutionBest forScore factor
512 by 512Older phones, tablets and weak integrated graphics0.6
720 by 720Modern phones, thin laptops, handheld PCs0.85
1024 by 1024The normal choice for comparing devices1.0
1440 by 1440Strong desktop and laptop GPUs1.4

A bigger canvas has more pixels, so the GPU has more rays to calculate. Doubling the width and height means four times as many pixels. That is why the same GPU can show a comfortable FPS at 512 and a slideshow at 1440.

For the preset, start with Mandelbulb. It is the classic scene that people mean when they talk about volume shader tests. The other presets are useful for curiosity, but if you want to compare with someone else, you must both use the same preset.

Tip: if your FPS sits exactly at 60 (or 120 or 144) and never moves, your screen refresh rate is probably the limit. Browsers do not draw faster than the screen. Raise the resolution until the number drops below the cap, and then you will see real differences between devices.

Free run or the 20 second benchmark

There are two ways to use the test, and they serve different goals.

Free run on the home page

Press the start button and the scene runs until you stop it. This is good for a quick look, for checking that WebGL works, and for watching how FPS changes as the device warms up. You can also pause, reset the view and toggle auto rotate.

The 20 second benchmark

This is the one to use when you want a number you can save or share. The page discards the first two seconds as warm up, records every frame for the rest of the run, and then shows a score card with average FPS, 1% low FPS, score and tier. You can copy a text summary or download the card as an image. There is also a dedicated benchmark page built around this routine.

Run it two or three times and compare. If all runs are close, you can trust the number. If the first run is clearly lower than the others, the cause is usually a cold start, because the browser has to compile the shader and the GPU may still be waking up from a low power state.

Reading the live numbers

While the scene is running, you see four live values. Here is how to think about each one.

  • FPS changes every moment. Do not judge your device from one glance at it.
  • Frame time is the other side of the same coin. 60 FPS means about 16.7 ms per frame, 30 FPS means about 33 ms. A smooth run shows a frame time that barely moves.
  • Average FPS is the steady picture over the whole run.
  • 1% low FPS is the speed of the slowest moments. If it is far below the average, the run had stutters.

The gap between average and 1% low is often more useful than either number alone. A small gap means a steady device. A big gap means something is interrupting the work, such as heat, a background app or a power setting.

How the score is calculated, with examples

The score is built from three things: average FPS, 1% low FPS and the resolution factor. The formula is:

score = (average FPS x 8.5 + 1% low FPS x 4.5) x resolution factor

The tier is then set from the score. A score of 950 or more is tier S, 600 or more is A, 350 or more is B, 180 or more is C, 80 or more is D, and anything lower is F.

Here are two runs at 1024 by 1024 with the same average speed but a different feel.

Run A (steady)Run B (stuttering)
Average FPS6060
1% low FPS5515
Calculation60 x 8.5 + 55 x 4.560 x 8.5 + 15 x 4.5
Score758578
TierAB

Both devices average the same speed, but the second one stutters, so it gets a lower score. This is on purpose. A smooth 60 feels very different from a 60 that keeps freezing.

The resolution factor makes scores at different sizes easier to put side by side, but it is only a rough correction. For a fair comparison, always use the same resolution on both devices.

What counts as a good result

There is no single magic number, because a phone and a desktop card are built for different jobs. These simple rules work well in practice.

  • Compare against yourself. Test your device now, then again after a driver update, a cleaning, a new thermal paste or a new case fan. The change is the real answer.
  • Compare similar devices. Two phones of the same age, or two laptops of the same size, are a fair match.
  • Look at stability first. A steady 30 FPS with a small gap to the 1% low is a healthier result than a wild 50 that drops to 10.
  • Use the comparison page as a rough guide only. Its numbers are estimates, not lab results.

Frequently asked questions

What is the volume shader test?+

It is a browser test that draws a 3D fractal with a heavy fragment shader and measures FPS, frame time, 1% low and a score. It is a quick way to see how fast a GPU can do shader math.

What is the difference between volume shader test and volumeshader_bm test?+

They are the same idea written in different ways. People search for volume shader test, volumeshader test, volume shader bm test and volumeshader_bm test when they want this kind of GPU test.

Which resolution should I choose?+

Use 1024 by 1024 for normal comparisons. Choose 512 for older phones and weak integrated graphics, and 1440 for strong desktop GPUs.

Why is the first run slower than the next ones?+

The browser must compile the shader, and the GPU may start from a low power state. The 20 second benchmark skips the first two seconds for this reason, but a second run is still often a little faster.

Why does my score change from run to run?+

Small changes are normal. Temperature, background tasks and power settings all move the result. If runs differ by a few percent, that is expected. Large differences point to heat or a background load.

How is the 1% low value calculated?+

After the run, all frame times are sorted. The page takes the frame time that 99 percent of frames are faster than, and converts it to FPS. It shows how bad the slow moments were.

Can I compare my score with scores from other websites?+

No. Different sites use different scenes, settings and formulas, so their numbers cannot be compared with each other. Only compare results made with the same test and the same settings.
